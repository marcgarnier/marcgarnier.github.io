The political construction of artificial intelligence in Canadian federal political discourse

A computational analysis of the federal policy process

1Object and research question

Since 2017, artificial intelligence has taken on a growing place in Canadian federal public action. This doctoral project studies how the discourse of Canadian parliamentarians frames the political stakes of artificial intelligence. Its object is therefore the framing process — not AI policy itself, nor the technology that makes it possible. One research question follows and sets the scope of the project:

How is artificial intelligence framed in Canadian political discourse, by which actors, and how do these framings change between 2017 and 2026?

Three axes follow from this question. The first concerns the thematic dimensions mobilised to talk about AI. The second focuses on the political actors who take up and use these different dimensions. The third addresses the shift in the hierarchy of frames over the period (2017–2026).

In the literature, the frames of an issue can delimit the range of solutions that appear possible and legitimate for addressing a problem in an executive or legislative setting (af Malmborg, 2023). Presenting AI as an economic opportunity for a country may call for investment and for a lighter touch on any regulation. Conversely, presenting it as a risk to fundamental rights calls for legal oversight. In a parliamentary arena, these discursive trade-offs shape the AI issue and its influence in the public sphere. Their translation into law, however, depends on institutional practices already in place (Hajer, 1993).

2Empirical context and time frame

The period I plan to cover begins in 2017 with the Pan-Canadian Artificial Intelligence Strategy, led by CIFAR and Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada. Earlier discussions of AI or robotics exist, but the Pan-Canadian Strategy is the Canadian state’s first real recognition of one of the major stakes in recent debates on AI: sovereignty. For the first time, the federal government treats AI as a fully fledged object of public policy, with its own funding, steering and discourse. 2017 is therefore the starting point of the corpus and the benchmark against which later developments will be measured.

Six milestones structure the period:

  • 2017

    Pan-Canadian Artificial Intelligence Strategy

    CIFAR / Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada

  • 2019

    Directive on Automated Decision-Making

    Treasury Board Secretariat

  • 2022

    Bill C-27 tabled: Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA)

    Parliament of Canada

  • 2024

    Public consultation on the national AI strategy

    Government of Canada

  • 2025

    AI Strategy for the Federal Public Service

    Treasury Board Secretariat

  • 2026

    National strategy “AI for All”

    Government of Canada

Beyond official initiatives, I expect to observe exogenous shocks: the mass diffusion of generative AI from late 2022 onwards, and the international summits devoted to AI governance.

3State of the art

Several studies show that framing is already studied within the policy process. In the context of policy practice, Schön and Rein (1994) define a frame as a diagnostic and prescriptive story, and distinguish rhetorical frames (reconstructed from the texts of a debate) from action frames (reconstructed from practices). Daviter (2011) offers a longitudinal study of biotechnology frames in the European policy process: the definition adopted for a policy issue determines which administrations take charge of it and which solutions are admissible. From another angle, Klüver, Mahoney and Opper (2015) examine interest groups and their framing choices to influence the European policy arena; they too argue that framing choices are shaped by the groups themselves and by the context in which they operate.

On AI and its regulation, Öge and Quintin (2026) follow how multiple actors — public institutions, interest groups and civil-society organisations — frame facial recognition in the United States and the European Union. Their study shows that frames shift over time, conditioning which regulations appear legitimate. Chueri and Törnberg (2026), for their part, analyse how political parties in 33 countries address the impact of AI on work, across 1.5 million parliamentary speeches from 2023 to 2026. Their method, very close to what I envisage for this thesis, combines speech extraction with automated content coding by a large language model.

3.1 Measuring framing computationally

Two bodies of work establish that framing can be measured rather than only interpreted. On the American death-penalty debate, Baumgartner, De Boef and Boydstun (2008) show that it is entirely possible to quantify the emergence of a new frame and the gradual displacement or replacement of earlier ones — the most direct precedent for the diachronic reasoning proposed here. Card et al. (2015) provide an annotation instrument that cuts across policy issues, whose structure inspires the scheme presented below.

Another close methodological precedent is the Canadian Climate Framing project (Lemor et al., 2026). It gathers 266,271 articles from twenty Canadian dailies between 1978 and 2024, segmented into 9.2 million sentence units and annotated along 65 hierarchical categories. BERT and CamemBERT classifiers are trained on a sample and validated through intercoder agreement (Cohen’s κ, Krippendorff’s α, Gwet’s AC1). The CCF project establishes the feasibility of supervised framing analysis on a large bilingual Canadian corpus. All of these studies, however, deal with media discourse, not with the discourse of politicians in a legislative setting.

3.2 Transformers applied to institutional political discourse

Three recent studies show that the institutional arena is accessible to these methods, without addressing the problem at hand here. Also in a European setting, Kos, Tuković, Vlainić and Bagić Babac (2026) apply BERT, RoBERTa and DistilBERT experimentally to parliamentary debates from 28 countries to classify ideological orientation (here, left versus right), using principal component analysis and attention scores to interpret cross-national regularities. This work shows that the approach works on parliamentary debates, not only on media corpora. Their task nonetheless remains simpler than mine: the classification is binary, and the labels come from distant supervision rather than human annotation.

In a doctoral thesis, Gupta (2023) applies BERT to 92 million tokens from about 170,000 British legislative and ministerial documents produced between 2000 and 2020. What interests me in this work is its architecture: it shows how an interpretive theoretical framework — the Foucauldian theory of language — can be linked to a natural language processing tool. The theoretical family I adopt differs, however. Gupta also shows that these techniques can be carried out on multimodal institutional corpora.

Lin (2025) goes further in the third essay of a doctoral thesis, detailing a procedure for identifying frames directly with GPT-4o through chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022), validated through crowdsourcing. The chain runs in three steps: quote (the model extracts passages and quotations from the original document), summarise (it condenses the extracts to bring out the main ideas) and synthesise (it turns this information into an abstract concept in the form of frame labels). This computational frame analysis outperformed a classic topic-modelling approach in several rounds of human validation.

Should one then train a supervised classifier on a human-annotated sample, or prompt a large language model directly? For now, I take the first route, for reasons of traceability and reproducibility (Do, Ollion & Shen, 2022). Lin’s framing analysis concerns a press corpus; the institutional corpus used in another part of that work, the Congressional Record, serves to measure message discipline. Using transformers, Lin notably finds that parties in power speak less homogeneously than those that do not have to govern.

Finally, Proulx et al. (2026) investigate how political frames of AI are constructed inside the European Parliament. Using a corpus of 666 plenary-session texts from 2014 to 2026 and a hybrid method combining topic discovery by a large language model with manual annotation, the authors show how AI discourse fits into fairly traditional ideological dynamics: right-wing MEPs favour techno-optimistic frames, left-wing MEPs techno-pessimistic ones. In line with one of the aims of this thesis, their work demonstrates the existence of exogenous shocks within the European institution, notably the post-ChatGPT period from 2022.

Work of this kind is still in its infancy, and the research community is only beginning to organise programmes that combine public policy and artificial intelligence, whether as an object or as a method. Among the studies above, none appears to follow a frame across the successive levels of a single institutional system, from external consultation to the text of the law. Nor does any appear to treat language as a variable. These are the two gaps this project addresses.

4Theoretical framework

Each dimension of the annotation scheme presented in section 7 is tied directly to an identified theoretical function. I adopt Entman’s (1993) definition: to frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a text, so as to promote a particular problem definition. Entman breaks the operation into four functions: defining a problem, diagnosing its causes, making a moral judgement and recommending a remedy.

To anchor this model in policy science, this structure is articulated with work on the argumentative turn in public policy, which shows that policy formulation is a discursive competition rather than a technical calculation (Fischer, 2003); with causal stories, which explain how the attribution of causes turns a technological fate into a problem for public action (Stone, 1989); with diagnostic and prescriptive frames (Schön & Rein, 1994); and with policy framing analysis in the governmental arena, which shows that the definition adopted for an issue determines which administrations take charge of the file, the regulatory or budgetary trade-offs, and the policy instruments deemed legitimate (Daviter, 2011).

A frame does not travel alone. Gamson and Modigliani (1989) show that it is carried by sponsors who invest resources to impose it in the public arena — an activity that Benford and Snow (2000) formalise as framing work. This literature grounds the actors dimension of the scheme and makes it possible to treat briefs submitted to committees as attempts to impose a frame. I distinguish issue-specific frames from generic frames, which transfer from one domain to another (De Vreese, 2005): the eight thematic frames are specific to AI, while the four evaluative framings are generic and comparable with other issues. I also distinguish frame-building from frame-setting, the effect of frames on individual representations (Scheufele, 1999). This thesis belongs to the first register only: it is concerned with the institutional production of frames, not their reception. Finally, the literature on message discipline, present in Lin (2025), makes it possible to theorise the specialisation of carriers: the institutional position and role of actors (ministers, backbench MPs, experts) constrain the consistency and variation of their framing work.

In this arena, framing has a direct legal consequence. The chain running from a brief submitted to a committee to a ministerial statement, then to the text of the law, can be observed within a single corpus — which grounds hypothesis H3, something a press corpus cannot reproduce. Klüver (2013) measured the passage from consultation briefs to European Commission proposals, and Leifeld and Haunss (2012) followed coalition frames through the European conflict over software patents. Neither, however, covers the full chain up to the adopted text. My hypothesis is that frames first emerge among external actors in briefs (strata 03 and 05 of the corpus), are debated in committee and in Parliament (stratum 01), taken up by ministers (stratum 04) and written into the text of the law (stratum 02). Tracking frames across the strata makes it possible to measure the diachronic shift in the hierarchy of frames (Baumgartner & Jones, 1993; Baumgartner et al., 2008). It also tests Lin’s (2025) hypothesis that initial ideological and normative debates gradually give way to more technical discussions centred on practical implementation.

This literature was built at the interface between the media and public policy, not within institutions (Anderson & Lawlor, 2016; Dodge & Metze, 2024). Transposing its analytical tools to parliamentary or governmental discourse therefore means changing the arena of action, not just the corpus. Such a move is not neutral: the validity of an instrument tested on journalistic text does not transfer automatically to institutional text, which is governed by distinct norms and rhetorical codes. Three theoretical consequences follow.

  • First

    The carrier of a frame becomes data

    In Hansard, the speaker is documented by name, party, riding, portfolio, committee and date of intervention. The actors dimension thus separates two objects that the media literature conflates — the speaker, available as exhaustive metadata, and the actors the speaker invokes — and measures the gap between who speaks and who is mobilised.

  • Second

    The chain from consultation to law is observable

    The path from external consultation to law can be followed continuously within a single archive, which is not the case in a media corpus.

  • Third

    Bilingualism becomes analytical

    Because Hansard is officially bilingual and translated, bilingualism ceases to be a sampling obstacle and becomes a theoretical question: is a frame invariant under official translation?

5Hypotheses

  • H1

    Shift in the dominant frame

    The economic frame, inherited from the industrial-policy logic of the 2017 Strategy, recedes in favour of regulatory and ethical framings from 2022 onwards, with the emergence of large language models. A relative share of the economic frame that remained stable over the period would refute the hypothesis.

  • H2

    Specialisation of carriers

    Evaluative framing covaries with the speaker’s institutional position, known from Hansard metadata: party affiliation and role as minister, committee member or backbench MP.

  • H3

    Discursive cascades

    A framing first appears among external actors, in briefs and advisory reports, before being taken up in committee, then by ministers, then written into the legislative text, with a measurable time lag.

  • H4

    Language as an invariant

    Because Hansard is officially bilingual and translated, the structure and distribution of policy frames do not vary under translation. A large gap between the distributions measured on the French and English versions of the same intervention would refute the hypothesis.

6Corpus

The corpus is organised into five strata. The first provides the volume needed to train the classifiers; the other four provide most of the analytical reach.

  • Stratum 01

    Parliamentary debates

    Hansard: House of Commons, committees (INDU, ETHI, SECU, RNNR), Senate. The main stratum, with an estimated 50,000 to 200,000 passages.

    Sources: ourcommons.ca, parl.ca (XML)

  • Stratum 02

    Strategic documents

    The 2017 Strategy, the Directive on Automated Decision-Making, AIDA (C-27), the 2026 strategy. The founding texts.

    Sources: canada.ca, ised-isde.canada.ca

  • Stratum 03

    Consultations and briefs

    Consultations on AIDA and on the national strategy, briefs submitted to committees. Reveals who seeks to weigh in from outside.

    Sources: consultations, canada.ca

  • Stratum 04

    Speeches and addresses

    Speeches from the Throne, federal budgets, ministerial addresses (ISED, Treasury Board Secretariat, Public Safety). Measures priority at the top of the state.

    Sources: canada.ca, archives

  • Stratum 05

    Advisory reports

    Advisory Council on Artificial Intelligence, Office of the Privacy Commissioner, Senate committees.

    Source: lop.parl.ca

The volumes indicated are estimates still to be confirmed. The passages actually related to AI will first need to be filtered; the criteria for this filter, and the bias of the lexical query chosen, will be the subject of a documented decision.

7Annotation scheme

Annotation is multi-label and operates at the sentence level. Its five dimensions are tied to theory as follows.

  • 8 categories

    Thematic frames

    Economic · Security · Ethics and rights · Scientific-technical · Regulation · Sovereignty · Labour · Health

    Entman’s (1993) first function: problem definition. Issue-specific frames (De Vreese, 2005). Structure adapted from the Media Frames Corpus (Card et al., 2015) and the CCF scheme (Lemor et al., 2026).

  • 4 categories

    Evaluative framings

    Opportunity · Risk · Urgency · Neutrality

    Entman’s (1993) third function: moral judgement. Generic frames (De Vreese, 2005).

  • 6 categories

    Actors and messengers

    Researchers · Civil servants · Businesses · Civil society · International organisations · Political parties

    Framing sponsors (Gamson & Modigliani, 1989); framing work (Benford & Snow, 2000).

  • 4 categories

    Causal attribution

    Fate · Technical cause · Negligence · Intentional cause

    Entman’s (1993) second function: causal attribution. Causal stories (Stone, 1989, p. 285) and focusing events.

  • 4 categories

    Policy action

    Regulate · Invest · Coordinate internationally · Abstain

    Entman’s (1993) fourth function: recommending a remedy. Prescriptive frames (Schön & Rein, 1994) and the legitimation of instruments in the governmental arena (Daviter, 2011).

The eight thematic frames are specific to the AI issue, and their structure draws on the general meta-frames of Card et al. (2015). The six actor categories capture the framing sponsors and the epistemic authorities invoked in discourse (Gamson & Modigliani, 1989; Benford & Snow, 2000). The four evaluative framings operationalise moral valence and the intensity of a statement as generic frames (De Vreese, 2005). Causal attribution is adapted from Stone (1989, p. 285).

A theme is not a frame

One objection should be anticipated. Identifying that a sentence talks about the economy is thematic analysis, not framing analysis. The multi-label structure answers this objection: the frame lies not in the thematic dimension alone, but in the pairing of a thematic dimension with an evaluative framing, attributed to a carrier. “Economic + opportunity” and “economic + risk” share a theme and form two opposing frames. Co-occurrence makes the frame.

The question of causal attribution also arises in this context. I expect the four positions Stone distinguishes to be salient in Canadian discourse on AI. Asserting that a technological revolution is under way, blaming discrimination on training data, or accusing a foreign actor of manipulating an election: these statements often share the same theme and the same evaluative framing, yet they designate different culprits and call for incompatible remedies. It is in this passage from an authorless attribution to one that names someone responsible that a technological development becomes a problem for public action (Stone, 1989).

8Methodological design

The design has four phases. The first three build an infrastructure; the fourth produces the political science results.

  • Phase 01

    Corpus construction

    Collecting the sources, filtering passages related to AI, sentence segmentation with spaCy, and structured storage in PostgreSQL with speaker and context metadata. This phase fixes the unit of analysis, a decision that cannot be revisited later.

  • Phase 02

    Building the gold standard

    This phase produces the examples without which no supervised learning is possible. It is the only point in the chain where human judgement enters, and everything that follows extrapolates from it. It unfolds in four steps. I first write the codebook: a definition of each category, positive examples, negative examples and borderline cases. Two people then independently code a subsample of 100 to 200 passages, stratified to over-represent rare labels. This coding is blind and done without automatic pre-labelling; otherwise both passes would anchor on the same suggestion and agreement would be inflated.

    Agreement is measured with Cohen’s κ and Gwet’s AC1, for each dimension and for each label taken as present or absent. Using two coefficients addresses a specific problem: Cohen’s κ collapses toward zero when a category is rare, even when raw agreement remains high. Gwet’s AC1 (2008) corrects this artefact by adjusting for prevalence. With multi-label tags, several of which will be infrequent, the structure of the data requires this pairing.

    A low score almost always signals a poorly defined category rather than failing coders. Repeated disagreement on “ethics and rights” indicates that its boundary with “regulation” has not been drawn. I then rewrite the faulty definition, date and version the revision, and run the test again on a fresh batch, since the same passages would measure the coders’ memory as much as the clarity of the definitions. The acceptability threshold, and the rule for a label that keeps failing, are set before the first test (Landis & Koch, 1977).

    Once agreement is judged satisfactory, I annotate the rest of the gold standard and split it into a training portion and a held-out portion. Annotation and supervised classification both take place at the sentence level, to capture precisely the multi-label co-occurrences between thematic dimensions and to guarantee the traceability of discursive cascades across the strata of the corpus.

  • Phase 03

    Supervised classification

    BERT for English and CamemBERT for French, or XLM-RoBERTa for both combined, are fine-tuned on the training portion, then evaluated on the held-out portion the model has never seen. This evaluation sets the level of confidence given to each label: a category whose F1 remains low cannot support any conclusion. F1 will be reported per label and as a macro average, since a micro average hides the failure of rare classes behind the success of frequent ones.

    The models are then applied to the whole corpus. This phase adds no knowledge; it only extends, at scale, a human judgement learned from about a thousand examples. I will compare several architectures, as the results of Kos et al. (2026) suggest that some offer a better trade-off between cost and accuracy. Since those results concern an English-language corpus, their transfer to French will have to be checked rather than assumed.

  • Phase 04

    Diachronic analysis

    This phase produces the statistical and diachronic synthesis of how AI is framed across the whole corpus. Sentence-level annotations are aggregated and crossed along four main axes: time (month and year), party affiliation, type of carrier and institutional stratum. Metrics are normalised as density per 1,000 words and aggregated by intervention, a unit delimited in the Hansard XML and attached to a single speaker, which preserves the link between a frame and its carrier. For strata without this markup, the unit of aggregation is the document section.

    The analysis measures realignments and shifts in the hierarchy of frames against the milestones of Canadian public action (the 2017 Strategy, the 2019 Directive, Bill C-27) as well as exogenous shocks. Finally, classifier performance (XLM-RoBERTa, BERT and CamemBERT) is systematically measured by time slice to detect any temporal drift of the model.

    Corpus and gold standard

    Phases 01 and 02: harvesting Hansard, filtering, segmentation and storage; codebook calibration and agreement measurement; gold-standard annotation and partitioning.

    Classification at scale

    Phase 04: diachronic analysis and discursive cascades; writing the chapters and submitting articles; thesis submission and defence.
